Basement Waterproofing Guide in Myrtle Beach, SC

A dry basement protects your home's structure, the air you breathe indoors, and what the property is worth. How Water Gets Into a Basement

Water gets into basements in several ways, and knowing the source is key to picking the right fix. Soaked soil presses on the foundation, and that hydrostatic pressure drives moisture through any crack, joint, or pore in the concrete. Clay soils swell when wet, and that sideways pressure can open cracks in a foundation wall where water then enters. Surface water from poor grading, clogged gutters, or weak drainage runs against your foundation and finds a way in. Condensation forms when warm, humid air meets cool basement surfaces, which creates dampness even with no active leak.

Outside Fixes for a Wet Basement

The most effective long-term waterproofing deals with water from the outside. The work involves digging out around the foundation, coating the walls with a waterproof membrane, and adding or improving the drain tile system outside. This option costs more than any other, yet it fixes the cause and protects the basement more completely than the alternatives. Proper grading, with soil sloping away from your foundation at least 1 inch per foot for the first 6 feet, is one of the most cost-effective exterior improvements.

Inside Fixes That Manage Water

Interior waterproofing systems manage water that gets through the foundation instead of keeping it from reaching the foundation. Water that reaches the basement perimeter is caught by an interior French drain, which carries it to the sump pump to be discharged. Sump pump systems, ideally with battery backup, keep water under control during heavy rain and power outages. Interior waterproof coatings and sealants give some protection against minor seepage and efflorescence, but they do not replace proper drainage when water intrusion is significant.

Sump Pump Care and Testing

A sump pump only protects a Myrtle Beach basement when it is maintained. Test your sump pump quarterly by pouring water into the pit until the pump turns on. Listen for unusual sounds that could point to mechanical problems. Because outages are when basement flooding is most common, a battery backup is worth installing. Consider a water-powered backup pump for extra redundancy. Check that discharged water lands a minimum of 6 feet from the house and that a check valve on the line stops backflow.
